NBA workload, shooting efficiency, and availability

A completed COGS 108 team research project.

Research question

Do NBA players who play heavier minutes during dense stretches of the 2025-2026 regular season show lower shooting efficiency in later games and lower availability later in the season?

The initial hypothesis predicted lower efficiency and availability after heavier workload. The reported results did not support that hypothesis.

My contribution

I was credited with project administration and experimental investigation. These roles connect team coordination with the investigation of our research question. The analysis and conclusions below describe the team's work, rather than claiming that I independently wrote every part.

Data and definitions

The report describes 26,651 player-game observations and 32 variables from NBA game logs. Important fields included minutes, game dates, points, shot attempts and shooting percentages.

Availability was measured through game-log appearance rate, not confirmed injury status. A missing appearance can reflect injury, rest, coaching choices, trades or role changes.

True shooting percentage was computed from points, field-goal attempts and free-throw attempts. Undefined shooting percentages associated with zero attempts were not automatically treated as corrupt data.

Analysis workflow

  1. Inspect the data types, duplicate records, missing values and impossible values.
  2. Construct workload, schedule and shooting-efficiency variables.
  3. Explore later-game efficiency and later-season appearance rates.
  4. Compare workload groups using Mann-Whitney U tests.
  5. Fit a linear regression controlling for first-half median shooting efficiency.

The team used Python, Pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, SciPy and Statsmodels.

Reported findings

Shooting efficiency

The group comparison found no statistically significant difference in second-half true shooting percentage (p = 0.678).

Later availability

The group with more heavy-dense first-half games had a higher reported second-half appearance rate: 0.817 versus 0.659 (p = 0.000026). This direction was opposite to the initial hypothesis.

Regression

First-half average minutes had a positive association with later appearance rate (coefficient = 0.0081, p < 0.001), controlling for first-half median true shooting percentage. The model explained little of the variation: R-squared = 0.063.

Interpretation and limitations

Selection bias is a plausible explanation: healthy, durable starters may both play more and remain available. This does not establish that heavy minutes improve durability. It also does not prove fatigue has no effect.

  • Only one season was analyzed.
  • Appearance rate is an imperfect proxy for health.
  • Age, position, travel and detailed injury history were not fully controlled.
  • The analysis is observational, so causal effects cannot be isolated.

What I learned

The original hypothesis is a starting point, not the desired answer. A useful analysis must report an unexpected direction, explain the limitations of its measures, and distinguish evidence from a possible explanation.

A follow-up study could add official injury reports, multiple seasons and richer player-level controls.
